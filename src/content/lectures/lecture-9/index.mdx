## 1 How Fitness Is Measured — and the Conflation Problem

<Note>
**Learning objectives:**
- The conflation of VO₂ max with cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF), and why it matters clinically
- CRF: definition, graded-exercise/MET measurement, and its status as a clinical vital sign
- Direct laboratory VO₂ max versus wearable estimation, and the accuracy gap between them
</Note>

### 1.1 Introduction and Clinical Context

**Clinical takeaway.** Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF), measured in metabolic equivalents of task (METs) on a treadmill or bicycle ergometer, is the evidence-based metric underpinning more than 99 % of the outcome literature linking physical fitness to all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. VO₂ max — especially as estimated by consumer wearables — is poorly validated, frequently inaccurate, and conflated with CRF in popular health narratives. METs are free, universally accessible, and clinically superior for most healthy adults.

Wearable fitness devices increasingly report VO₂ max values to consumers, prompting anxiety and clinical inquiries when those values decline. Topol (2026) describes two recent patients who contacted him concerned about a falling VO₂ max displayed by their smartwatches — a scenario that illustrates a far broader problem: the widespread conflation of **VO₂ max** (maximal oxygen uptake) with **cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF)**, two related but methodologically and evidentially distinct constructs.

This lecture distils that critical analysis into an evidence-based framework covering: (1) how each metric is defined and measured; (2) the strength and size of the supporting outcome datasets; (3) the origin and consequences of conflation in popular media; and (4) practical recommendations grounded in peer-reviewed evidence. The figures throughout are reconstructed from published data; they are not reproductions of the original article graphics.

### 1.2 Cardiorespiratory Fitness (CRF)

<Figure src={fig1} srcDark={fig1Dark} alt="Measurement Comparison: CRF/METs vs VO₂ Max vs Wearable">
Conceptual comparison of CRF/METs vs. VO₂ max measurement approaches. CRF can be assessed freely on any treadmill or ergometer, requires no specialist, and represents >99 % of the mortality outcome literature. VO₂ max requires a calibrated metabolic cart, exhaled-gas mask, and trained staff.
</Figure>

CRF reflects the integrated capacity of the cardiovascular, pulmonary, and musculoskeletal systems to sustain aerobic exercise (Ross et al. 2016). It is assessed via graded exercise testing and expressed as metabolic equivalents of task (METs), where 1 MET equals the resting metabolic rate.

<Figure
	src={fig2a}
	srcDark={fig2aDark}
	alt="MET Intensity Classification"
	panels={[{ src: fig2b, srcDark: fig2bDark, alt: 'MET Heart-Rate Estimation Formula' }]}
>
MET intensity classification and heart-rate estimation formula. MET ≈ 0.05 × HR_bpm + 2; every 10-bpm HR increment corresponds to approximately 1 additional MET. Light &lt;3, Moderate 3–6, Vigorous >6 METs (Topol 2026).
</Figure>

The American Heart Association classifies CRF as a **clinical vital sign** (Ross et al. 2016).

### 1.3 VO₂ Max — Direct Laboratory Measurement

Maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂ max) is the ceiling of aerobic power, determined via *direct gas exchange* during a ramp-protocol exercise test to volitional exhaustion (Topol 2026). Requirements: calibrated metabolic cart, tight mask, and trained exercise physiologist. Cost: ~$150 per test. Result expressed in mL O₂ · kg⁻¹ · min⁻¹. These requirements make routine clinical screening impractical.

### 1.4 VO₂ Max — Wearable Estimation

<Figure
	src={fig3a}
	srcDark={fig3aDark}
	alt="Wearable Accuracy"
	panels={[{ src: fig3b, srcDark: fig3bDark, alt: 'Wearable Accuracy' }]}
>
Consumer smartwatches impute VO₂ max from population-based algorithms (heart rate + GPS/accelerometry), not gas exchange (Tedesco et al. 2025). Accuracy is poor: mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) is typically 7–16 %.
</Figure>

Because wearables estimate rather than measure VO₂ max, their values carry directional biases and extrinsic confounders that make them unreliable for clinical or lifestyle decisions.

### 2.1 Meta-Analysis: 33 Studies, 102,980 Participants (JAMA 2009)

<Figure src={fig4} srcDark={fig4Dark} alt="CRF and Mortality Risk — JAMA 2009">
Cardiorespiratory fitness and mortality risk. Each 1-MET increment in exercise capacity is associated with a **14–15 % reduction in all-cause mortality**. Individuals achieving ≥7.9 METs showed markedly lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Shaded area = 95 % CI. Meta-analysis of 33 studies (n = 102,980; Kodama et al. 2009).
</Figure>

The landmark meta-analysis by Kodama et al. (2009) established METs as a potent and graded prognostic variable for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality.

All-cause mortality hazard ratios by fitness category, Cleveland Clinic cohort. > **Cleveland Clinic Key Results**
>
> - Five MET strata: Low, Below-Average, Above-Average, High, Elite.
> - HR (above-average vs. below-average): **1.41** — equivalent to **smoking or diabetes**.
> - HR (Low vs. Elite): **>5-fold**.
> - Protective effect **more pronounced in women** at every stratum.
> - **No ceiling mortality risk** even at elite fitness.

Across all three datasets, the fitness–survival relationship is **graded and continuous with no ceiling**: higher CRF is associated with lower mortality at every level, and the largest absolute gains accrue at the low-fitness end of the spectrum.

## 3 The VO₂ Max Verdict and Practical Recommendations

<Note>
**Learning objectives:**

- The similar ~14 % effect as construct validity — and why it does not license wearable VO₂ max
- Metric-by-metric recommendations, and the clinical niches where direct VO₂ max retains value
</Note>

### 3.1 Meta-Analysis: 42 Studies, CRF vs. VO₂ Max (2024)

<Figure
	src={fig7a}
	srcDark={fig7aDark}
	alt="Meta-Analysis 2024: Participants"
	panels={[
		{ src: fig7b, srcDark: fig7bDark, alt: 'Meta-Analysis 2024: Studies' },
		{ src: fig7c, srcDark: fig7cDark, alt: 'Meta-Analysis 2024: Effect Size' },
	]}
>
Data imbalance between METs-based CRF and direct VO₂ max evidence. **a**: >99 % of outcome-linked participants contributed MET data; &lt;1 % contributed direct VO₂ max data. **b**: Study count comparison. **c**: Point estimates for cardiovascular mortality reduction are remarkably similar (≈14 %), confirming construct validity — but the evidence base rests almost entirely on METs (Swain and Franklin 2024; 234-fold more participants in the MET arm).
</Figure>

> **Critical Finding: Data Imbalance**
>
> **234-fold more participants** contributed data via METs than via direct VO₂ max measurement. Over 99 % of all evidence linking fitness to mortality derives from **METs-based CRF** — not VO₂ max.

The evidence base is profoundly imbalanced. The 2024 meta-analysis by Swain and Franklin (2024), comparing CRF and VO₂ max across 42 studies, revealed that 234-fold more study participants contributed METs-based CRF data than direct VO₂ max data. This asymmetry reflects both the practical accessibility of METs testing and the specialized requirements for direct VO₂ max measurement. The near-identical ≈14 % effect estimates confirm **construct validity** — the two metrics capture the same underlying aerobic capacity — but this similarity does *not* justify replacing an accessible, well-evidenced measure (METs) with an inaccurate wearable estimate of the other.

### 3.2 Specific Clinical Applications of VO₂ Max

In selected clinical populations, direct VO₂ max measurement has demonstrated utility: in heart failure for risk stratification and transplant listing; in pulmonary hypertension as a prognostic marker (Topol 2026); and in pre-operative evaluation for predicting post-surgical cardiopulmonary complications (Older et al. 1993).

### 3.3 Practical Recommendations

<Table>

| **Metric** | **Recommendation** |
|---|---|
| **METs (CRF)** | **Preferred for most healthy adults.** Free, universally accessible, best studied for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality outcomes (>99 % of evidence base). Use treadmill/bicycle speed-incline charts, Bruce protocol, or HR formula. |
| **VO₂ Max (lab)** | Reserve for: (a) high-performance athletes; (b) heart failure/pulmonary hypertension risk stratification; (c) pre-operative evaluation. |
| **Wearable VO₂ Max** | **Not recommended** for clinical or lifestyle decision-making. MAPE 7–16 %, directional biases, and extrinsic confounders render it unreliable. |
| **Strength & Balance** | Include alongside CRF. Combined aerobic + strength training is superior to aerobic training alone for mortality reduction (Kim et al. 2023). |

</Table>

Topol's bottom line: *"Perhaps the better strategy is to be as physically active as possible and **not worry about any metric**!"* (Topol 2026). The emphasis should be on building a comprehensive, enjoyable activity habit rather than chasing a single number from a device whose accuracy is demonstrably limited.

### 3.4 Summary Comparison: CRF vs. VO₂ Max

<Table>

| **Dimension** | **CRF / METs** | **VO₂ Max** |
|---|---|---|
| Measurement | Treadmill/bike; HR formula | Direct gas exchange in metabolic lab |
| Cost | Free | ≈$150/test |
| Accessibility | Universal | Specialist lab only |
| Wearable estimate | Indirect (step count, HR) | Algorithmic imputation (MAPE 7–16 %) |
| Evidence base | >750,000 per large study | &lt;1,300 per study (direct measure) |
| Fraction of outcome data | >99 % | &lt;1 % |
| AHA recommendation | Clinical vital sign | Niche clinical/sports use |
| Target population | All healthy adults | Elite athletes; HF/PH patients; pre-op |
